The Political Geography of America

Do political parties compete for every citizen—or concentrate their presidential campaigns in a handful of states?

Red, blue, and swing are campaign classifications, not permanent identities assigned to the citizens of a state. This research examines how presidential campaign attention developed and why geography matters under the Electoral College.

Explore the evidence View primary sources

The central distinction

What is documented

Presidential campaign events in recent elections were concentrated overwhelmingly in a small number of competitive states.

What is not established

That the major parties secretly agreed to divide states into permanent political territories. Similar incentives can produce similar campaign strategies without an agreement.

What deserves examination

Whether a battleground-focused system limits the opportunities for citizens outside competitive states to interact directly with presidential candidates.

Historical milestones

1960 · A nationwide campaign example

Nixon stated that he had visited all 50 states. Kennedy also campaigned broadly, while candidates weighed electoral significance and travel demands. Even this early example did not mean attention was equal in every state.

1960s–1980s · Party coalitions shift

Southern, suburban, and regional voting patterns changed over multiple elections. Modern state party identities cannot be projected unchanged backward through American history.

2000 · Red and blue become familiar shorthand

Television electoral maps popularized the now-familiar party-color convention during the closely contested presidential contest. The colors represent the winner in a jurisdiction, not unanimity among its voters.

2004–2024 · Competitive states dominate campaign schedules

Studies of campaign events repeatedly show geographic concentration. FairVote's 2024 tracker provides a concrete, inspectable example; counts and definitions should be checked separately for each election.

Why does the Electoral College shape campaigning?

The National Archives explains that 48 states and Washington, D.C. allocate all their electoral votes to the statewide winner; Maine and Nebraska use a district-based method for some electoral votes. Under winner-take-all allocation, a close win can earn the same electoral-vote total as a large win in the same state.

Under this system, presidential campaigns have incentives to devote attention to states where additional votes could change the statewide winner. This explains a strategic incentive, not the intentions of every individual campaign.

Questions citizens can investigate

Visits

Where did presidential and vice-presidential candidates appear in person, and during which tracking period?

Votes

What percentage and number of voters supported each candidate within each state?

Representation

How do citizens in states receiving fewer candidate visits communicate their concerns and evaluate officeholders between elections?

Campaign visits, electoral outcomes, and presidential governance are separate research questions. A lack of campaign visits does not, by itself, prove that a candidate or elected president ignored a state's interests.

No State Belongs to a Political Party. Every State Belongs to Its Citizens.

Red states and blue states are descriptions of election results—not constitutional identities. Civic participation and public accountability belong to every citizen, regardless of geography or party.
