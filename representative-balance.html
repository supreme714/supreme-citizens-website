Restoring Representative Balance

The financial worth of a citizen should never determine the civic worth of their voice.

Explore the relationship between corporate rights, political expression, financial influence and representative accountability.

Freedom to speak and the capacity to be heard

Corporate personhood

Corporations are legal entities with certain constitutional protections, not voting citizens.

Financial amplification

Spending can increase the reach of political expression. Unequal resources do not guarantee unequal outcomes, but can create unequal opportunities for access and visibility.

Representative equity

One person, one vote does not imply equal financial capacity to influence political debate.

Transparency

Super PACs report qualifying direct contributors; some intermediary organizations do not publicly identify their original donors. Straw-donor concealment is unlawful.

How funding can become less transparent

Direct funding:

Original contributor → Super PAC → Public disclosure

Indirect funding:

Original donor → Nonprofit intermediary → Super PAC → Intermediary disclosed; original donor may remain unknown

Illustrative only. Actual disclosure duties depend on the purpose and circumstances of the transfer.

Constitutional considerations

Buckley v. Valeo (1976) protected spending as a means of political expression while upholding certain contribution limits. Citizens United (2010) protected independent corporate and union expenditures and upheld challenged disclosure requirements. Americans for Prosperity Foundation v. Bonta (2021) emphasized narrow tailoring of compelled disclosure.

Supporters of expenditure protections emphasize free expression and association; critics emphasize the risks of concentrated political influence. Disclosure reforms must account for both electoral transparency and legitimate privacy interests.

Proposed supplemental declaration

Declaration of Representative Equality and Civic Voice

We, the People, recognize that governmental authority derives from the consent of the governed, not the accumulation of private wealth. Freedom of expression, representative equality and public accountability should coexist.

Article I — Responsible Corporate Citizenship

Corporate rights and protections should be accompanied by reciprocal responsibilities toward the people and institutions that enable corporate prosperity.

Article II — Representative Equality

Every citizen should possess equal representative standing regardless of wealth. Contributions should not confer a superior claim to representation.

Article III — Equal Civic Voice

Money can communicate and amplify expression; it is not a measure of its inherent worth. A citizen’s inability to pay should not diminish civic standing.

Article IV — Transparency of Political Influence

The public has a legitimate interest in knowing who finances substantial election-related activity, subject to constitutional privacy safeguards.

Article V — Representative Integrity

The absence of criminal corruption does not necessarily establish the absence of representative distortion.

Article VI — Accountability to the People

Representatives serve constituents regardless of their ability to finance campaigns. Contributions confer no ownership of public office.

Article VII — Preservation of Constitutional Liberty

Reforms should respect speech, association, legitimate privacy and other constitutional protections.

One people. Equal civic standing. Accountable representation.

This supplemental declaration does not amend the Charter’s original articles or principles and is not existing law.

120th Congress · Proposed discussion draft

Political Financial Transparency and Representative Integrity Act of 2027

Not an introduced bill or enacted law. The following is a section-by-section summary of the proposed legislation; thresholds are proposed drafting choices. The proposal does not impose new limits on lawful independent political expenditures.

  1. Short title

    Political Financial Transparency and Representative Integrity Act of 2027.

  2. Congressional findings

    Political expression is protected; financial capacities differ. Concealed election funding can impair informed electoral decisions. Transparency must coexist with freedom of association and privacy.

  3. Purpose

    Strengthen original-source disclosure, prevent unlawful concealment, improve timely public access and protect constitutional liberties.

  4. Definitions

    Covered organizations include Super PACs, hybrid PAC non-contribution accounts and other covered spenders. Covered activity includes independent expenditures, electioneering communications and qualifying transfers. Original sources are persons or entities designating, directing, or knowingly supplying funds for covered activity. Covered transfers aggregate $10,000 or more per calendar year.

  5. Original-source disclosure

    Obtain and report reasonably available original-source identities, attributable amounts, dates, known intermediaries and certifications. Aggregate intentional threshold evasion; require reasonable due diligence. Identify organizations using legitimately earned treasury revenue as the source, subject to anti-evasion rules. Preserve existing disclosure obligations.

  6. Segregated accounts

    Permit dedicated political accounts and protect unrelated nonpolitical donors. FEC accounting standards should prevent circumvention.

  7. Timely reporting

    Report qualifying transfers aggregating $50,000 or more within seven days, or 48 hours during the 60 days preceding the relevant federal election. Existing deadlines remain applicable.

  8. Transparency portal

    Expand free searchable, downloadable, machine-readable FEC records showing original sources, intermediaries, recipients, amounts, dates, expenditures and candidate support or opposition where applicable; avoid duplicate accounting and protect sensitive data.

  9. Disclaimers

    Provide reasonable access to source disclosures through links or reference numbers, tailored to communication formats; retain existing disclaimers.

  10. Intentional concealment

    Prohibit knowing and willful straw-donor schemes, false source reports, intentional evasion and obstruction through falsification or destruction of required records. Legitimate transfers are not violations merely because intermediaries participate.

  11. Privacy and constitutional rights

    Limit disclosure to information necessary for electoral transparency; prohibit viewpoint discrimination; provide expedited confidential-treatment requests for credible threats or reprisals and administrative and judicial review. Exclude unrelated donors and sensitive identifiers.

  12. Enforcement

    FEC administration and authorized investigations, proportionate civil penalties, DOJ referral where appropriate, and correction opportunities for inadvertent errors; enforce without regard to viewpoint.

  13. Whistleblower protection

    Protect employees reporting reasonably suspected violations lawfully to authorized agencies; provide appropriate remedies including reinstatement and lost compensation, subject to further legislative drafting.

  14. Congressional review

    Biennial GAO public assessment of funding concentration, transfers, compliance, enforcement and constitutional burdens, without judging viewpoints.

  15. Rule of construction

    No new limits on lawful independent expenditures; preserve lawful expression, existing contribution and coordination rules, and unrelated donor privacy. No corporate voting rights.

  16. Severability

    Preserve remaining valid provisions if a provision is held unconstitutional.

  17. Appropriations

    Authorize sums necessary for administration, enforcement and modernization.

  18. Effective date

    Proposed rulemaking within 180 days of enactment; substantive obligations effective after 365 days, prospectively.

Legislative counsel would need to prepare precise statutory amendments and assess constitutional and implementation issues. The original-source definition focuses on funds knowingly provided for election-related activity and would not necessarily reveal every general-purpose nonprofit donor.
