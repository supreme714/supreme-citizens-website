Part VII · Memory, Record & Republic

PEOPLE, VOICES
& ORAL HISTORIES

Documents establish part of the historical record. People preserve another part: what they saw, heard, believed, feared, decided and experienced. This section preserves those accounts while distinguishing personal memory from independently established fact.

The governing rule

A memory is a source. It is not automatically a fact.

First-person testimony can provide information unavailable anywhere else. It can also be incomplete, mistaken, influenced by later events or contradicted by other evidence. The project therefore preserves testimony without automatically adopting every recollection as an established historical fact.

Respect the witness. Preserve the account. Test the account against the record.

Official January 6 records include public testimony, transcribed interviews, depositions, hearing videos and supporting documents. Those materials provide an important foundation for this section, but the source and procedural setting of each statement must remain visible to readers.

Oral-history standard

Every account should carry its own context

Who is speaking?

Identify the person, their role on or around January 6, and why their account may be relevant.

Where were they?

Identify the location and whether the person directly observed the event being described.

When was the account given?

Distinguish a contemporaneous statement from an interview, deposition or recollection given months or years later.

Was it under oath?

Identify testimony given under oath, formal transcribed interviews, public interviews and informal recollections separately.

What supports it?

Connect video, audio, documents, communications, other witnesses or records that corroborate material portions of the account.

What conflicts with it?

Present meaningful contrary evidence, conflicting recollections or unresolved discrepancies rather than hiding them.

Whose voices belong in the record?

The history is larger than any one institution or political viewpoint

Members of Congress & congressional staff
Accounts from people participating in, supporting or observing the electoral count.
Vice President & staff
Accounts concerning constitutional responsibilities, security and the continuation of the joint session.
U.S. Capitol Police
First-person accounts from officers responsible for protecting the Capitol complex.
Metropolitan Police & other responders
Accounts from officers and emergency personnel who responded to requests for assistance.
Election officials
State and local officials involved in administration, certification, recounts and election-related disputes.
Trump administration officials
Accounts from White House, Justice Department, Defense Department and other executive-branch personnel.
Military & National Guard personnel
Accounts relevant to command, authorization, mobilization and deployment.
Demonstrators who did not enter the Capitol
Accounts from people who came to Washington to demonstrate but did not enter the building.
People who entered the Capitol
Accounts preserved with the person's conduct and any applicable legal record clearly identified.
Defendants & former defendants
Statements, testimony and recollections considered alongside charging documents, adjudications and later legal developments.
Journalists, filmmakers & photographers
Accounts from people documenting events in real time and preserving visual or audio evidence.
Families & personal consequences
Accounts describing injury, loss, trauma and other personal consequences, clearly distinguished from findings about legal responsibility.

Oral-history record

A standard profile for every voice

Voice ID: J6-V-0001

Person: Name and relevant role

Location on January 6: Known location or operational assignment

Source: Hearing / deposition / interview / contemporaneous statement / other

Date of account: Date the testimony or recollection was recorded

Under oath: Yes / No / Not applicable

Direct or secondhand: What the speaker personally observed versus what was reported to them

Related evidence: Video, audio, documents, communications and other testimony

Corroboration: Evidence materially supporting the account

Conflicting evidence: Material discrepancies or contrary accounts

Related legal status: If applicable, clearly separated from the historical account

Reader note: What the account can and cannot establish by itself

How testimony becomes evidence

One account can contain several different kinds of information

Statement typeHow the project treats it
“I saw…”Direct observation, evaluated against location, timing and available records.
“I heard…”May be direct auditory evidence or a secondhand report; the distinction is recorded.
“I believed…”Evidence of the speaker's state of mind, not automatically proof that the underlying belief was true.
“Someone told me…”Secondhand account requiring identification and corroboration where possible.
“I remember…”Later recollection preserved with the time elapsed and any corroborating or conflicting evidence.
“The record shows…”A factual assertion that should be linked directly to the underlying record.
Conflicting memories

Disagreement belongs in the record

Two sincere witnesses can remember the same event differently. A witness can also change, clarify or correct an earlier account. When accounts materially conflict, the project should not silently choose one version.

Account A

Present the relevant statement with its source, date and evidentiary setting.

Account B

Present the conflicting statement under the same standards.

Independent record

Compare both accounts with video, documents, timestamps, communications, other testimony and adjudicated findings where available.

Multiple perspectives do not require artificial balance. A well-supported account and an unsupported account should not be presented as equally established merely because they conflict. The evidence determines the weight, while the project preserves the existence of the disagreement.
Public submissions

Preserve memories without weakening the evidence standard

A future submission system could allow witnesses, participants, officials, responders, journalists and members of the public to offer materials or accounts for preservation. Submission alone should never make an account authenticated evidence.

Submission

Receive the account and any supporting files with consent and contact information handled under a published privacy policy.

Verification

Evaluate identity, provenance, dates, metadata and supporting records before assigning an authenticated evidence status.

Publication

Publish only with appropriate permissions, contextual notes, source classification and protections for sensitive personal information.

Anonymous material: anonymous submissions may provide research leads, but should not automatically enter the authenticated Evidence Ledger as established evidence.
