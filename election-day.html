Election Day Belongs to the People.

We make time for the occasions we value. What if America also made Election Day a national civic tradition—shared with family, friends, neighbors, and the next generation?

WE MAKE TIME FOR WHAT WE VALUE

What if we celebrated citizenship together?

We gather for Thanksgiving. We celebrate Independence Day with family and friends. We honor sacrifice on Memorial Day and service on Veterans Day. Religious, cultural, and family holidays become meaningful because we do more than recognize them on a calendar—we participate in them together.

Citizenship deserves a tradition of its own.

Election Day can be an opportunity for Americans to celebrate participation in self-government without celebrating a political party, candidate, ideology, or electoral outcome. We do not have to vote alike to participate together.

THE CIVIC CYCLE

Election Day is the destination—not the beginning.

Before Election Day

Learn · Discuss · Prepare

Research candidates and ballot questions, understand the offices being decided, use reliable election information, and make a voting plan.

On or Before Election Day

Participate · Vote

Use the lawful voting method available to you. Celebrate first-time voters, thank election workers, and encourage eligible family and friends to participate.

After Election Day

Stay Informed · Stay Engaged

Follow results and certification, learn who represents you, communicate with officials, attend public meetings, serve your community, and prepare for the next election.

OUR FAMILY PARTICIPATES

Create an Election Day family tradition.

Election Day Breakfast

Begin the day together and talk about why participation matters.

First Vote Celebration

Recognize a family member casting a ballot for the first time.

Vote Together

When practical, family members, couples, friends, or neighbors can make participation a shared activity.

Ask the Elders

Invite grandparents and older relatives to share memories of their first vote and elections they remember.

Teach the Next Generation

Explain elections, the privacy of the ballot, respectful disagreement, and the responsibility future voters will inherit.

Election Night Dinner

Gather after voting. Follow the results together and remember that relationships and shared citizenship continue even when political choices differ.

THE NATIONAL CIVIC HOLIDAY PROPOSAL

Recognize the federal general Election Day as a legal public holiday.

The Supreme Citizens Charter proposes adding the Tuesday next after the first Monday in November in each even-numbered year to the legal public holidays recognized in 5 U.S.C. § 6103.

Model Legislative Framework

Short title: Election Day National Civic Holiday Act.

Core amendment: Amend 5 U.S.C. § 6103(a) to add: “Election Day, the Tuesday next after the first Monday in November in each even-numbered year.”

Civic purpose: Encourage informed participation, civic education, intergenerational traditions, recognition of election workers, and continuing engagement after elections.

Nonpartisan safeguard: Official observances should promote citizenship and participation, not endorse or oppose a candidate, party, ideology, or electoral outcome.

Essential services: Recognition of a federal holiday does not eliminate the need for healthcare, public safety, transportation, emergency response, national security, utilities, election administration, and other continuous services.

Voting methods: The proposal does not express a preference against lawful early, absentee, or mail voting. The civic tradition celebrates participation regardless of the lawful method used.

A federal legal public holiday directly governs federal holiday observance; it does not by itself guarantee that every private-sector employee will receive the day off. State election laws and voting options vary.

PUBLIC DECLARATION

We make time for what we value.

We celebrate our families. We celebrate our traditions. We celebrate our history. We celebrate independence. We honor sacrifice. We celebrate the people we love.

Let us also celebrate our role as citizens.

We envision an Election Day when families make voting plans together, first-time voters are celebrated, grandparents share their voting stories, parents teach children about representative government, friends encourage friends to participate, and Americans gather knowing that—even when we disagree—we participated together.

Election Day does not belong to a political party. Election Day belongs to the people.
