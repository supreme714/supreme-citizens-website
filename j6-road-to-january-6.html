Part II · Memory, Record & Republic

THE ROAD TO JANUARY 6

November 3, 2020 — January 5, 2021

January 6 did not arise in isolation. The weeks after the 2020 presidential election included state canvassing and certification, recounts, litigation, competing legal and political arguments, the meeting of presidential electors, congressional plans to object to electoral votes, and a dispute over the Vice President’s role. This timeline separates what happened from what was alleged, argued, adjudicated or left unresolved.

Evidence-first chronology

From Election Day to the eve of the count

NOVEMBER 3, 2020

Election Day

Primary record

Voters cast presidential ballots under election laws administered by the states. Election night did not itself complete the constitutional process; states still had to canvass results, resolve authorized recounts and challenges, certify results and appoint electors.

NOVEMBER 2020

Challenges, recounts and litigation begin

LitigationAllegations

President Donald Trump, his campaign, allied organizations and other plaintiffs challenged aspects of election administration and results in several states. Claims varied substantially by case. A filing or allegation is not itself a judicial finding, so each case must be evaluated by what was alleged, what evidence was accepted, what issue the court actually decided and the final disposition.

NOVEMBER–DECEMBER 2020

States complete their election processes

Official state record

States continued canvassing, certification, recount and litigation procedures under their own laws. The project will preserve state-level records separately so that a certification, recount result, audit, lawsuit and political assertion are not treated as interchangeable forms of evidence.

DECEMBER 8, 2020

A key Electoral Count Act milestone

Federal law in 2020

The pre-2022 Electoral Count Act contained timing provisions connected to state determinations of electoral disputes. Because Congress later revised federal electoral-count law, this project distinguishes the rules governing the 2020 election and January 6, 2021 from the law governing future counts.

DECEMBER 11, 2020

Texas v. Pennsylvania

Supreme Court disposition

Texas sought permission to bring an original action against Pennsylvania, Georgia, Michigan and Wisconsin. The Supreme Court denied Texas’s motion for leave to file because Texas had not demonstrated a judicially cognizable interest in how another state conducts its elections.

What the ruling did — and did not — decide

The Court disposed of the case on standing. It did not conduct a trial resolving every underlying election allegation on the merits. Keeping procedural dispositions separate from merits adjudications is central to this project’s evidence standard.

DECEMBER 14, 2020

Presidential electors meet

Official electoral record

Appointed electors met in their respective states and cast electoral votes. The official Electoral College result ultimately recorded by the National Archives was 306 electoral votes for Joseph R. Biden Jr. and 232 for Donald J. Trump.

Examine the National Archives 2020 Electoral College record →
DECEMBER 14, 2020

Competing elector documents

Primary documentsLater litigation

In several states won by Biden, groups of Republicans signed documents purporting to cast electoral votes for Trump. These documents became important to later investigations and litigation. The Source Vault will place official state certificates, competing documents and subsequent legal records side by side so visitors can examine their wording, provenance and claimed legal basis.

MID-DECEMBER 2020 — JANUARY 2021

The Vice President’s authority becomes a central dispute

Constitutional dispute

Arguments developed over whether the Vice President, while presiding over the joint session, possessed authority to reject, delay or otherwise alter the treatment of electoral votes. Part III will show the arguments alongside the Twelfth Amendment, the law then in effect, Vice President Pence’s January 6 statement and later legal developments.

LATE DECEMBER 2020 — JANUARY 2021

Members of Congress announce electoral objections

Congressional process

Members of Congress announced plans to object to electoral votes during the January 6 joint session. Under the law governing the 2021 count, a written objection signed by at least one Representative and one Senator could cause the chambers to separate and consider the objection. This statutory procedure is analytically distinct from the later breach of the Capitol.

JANUARY 2, 2021

Trump–Raffensperger Georgia call

Recorded communicationDisputed interpretation

President Trump spoke by telephone with Georgia Secretary of State Brad Raffensperger and others about Georgia’s election result and disputed claims concerning the vote. Because excerpts can remove context, the project’s Evidence Ledger will prioritize the complete recording, transcript and related official records when examining the call.

JANUARY 3–5, 2021

Congress and Washington prepare for January 6

Official preparations

Congress prepared for the joint session while demonstrations were planned in Washington and federal, local and Capitol security organizations prepared for events around the city. Part IV will separately examine what intelligence existed, who received it, what actions followed and what later investigations concluded.

JANUARY 5, 2021

The eve of the joint session

Historical context

By the evening of January 5, several distinct processes were converging: the scheduled congressional count, planned electoral objections, continuing political pressure concerning the Vice President’s role, public demonstrations and security preparations. The next part follows January 6 itself through synchronized timelines.

A critical distinction

Disagreement, allegation and adjudication are not the same thing.

A historical record becomes less reliable when every lawsuit is described as a proven claim, every dismissal is described as a factual exoneration, or every political statement is treated as an official finding. This project records the claim, the evidence offered, the legal question actually decided and the final disposition.

Record standard: Primary and official records are preferred. Later pages will add source-by-source evidence cards and correction history. Historical descriptions will be updated when reliable evidence materially changes the record.
