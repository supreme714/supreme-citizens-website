How legislation moves

The Legislative Activity Pipeline

The full tracker is organized around observable stages in the federal legislative process.

01

Introduced

A bill or resolution is formally introduced in the House or Senate.

02

Committee Action

Committees may consider, report, amend, or otherwise act on legislation.

03

Chamber Passage

The House or Senate approves a measure. Passage by one chamber does not itself make law.

04

Both Chambers

For enactment, both chambers must agree to the same legislative text.

05

Presented

An enrolled bill is presented to the President after congressional passage.

06

Became Law

A measure may become law through presidential approval or other constitutional procedures.

07

Calendar Time

Shows ordinary elapsed days since a measurable legislative event.

08

Session Time

Shows how many official chamber session days occurred after an event, including recorded pro forma sessions.

How to read this scoreboard: Legislative activity is measurable, but activity alone does not establish whether legislation is good, bad, necessary, unnecessary, bipartisan, partisan, effective, or constitutional. A high number of bills is not automatically evidence of good government, and a low number is not automatically evidence of failure. Citizens should use the tracker as a starting point for examining the substance, consequences, votes, and constitutional basis of legislation.

Official Data

The tracker uses Congress.gov legislative and Daily Congressional Record data. Congress.gov provides bill, action, law, congressional session, and Congressional Record endpoints for public use.

Open Congress.gov

Why calendar days and session days?

Calendar days show elapsed public time. Session days add context by showing how many days the House or Senate actually convened after an event. The Congressional Record is published for days when one or both chambers are in session, subject to limited publication exceptions.
