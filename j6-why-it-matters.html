Part I · Memory, Record & Republic

WHY JANUARY 6 MATTERS

Elections, the transfer of presidential power, and the continuity of the Republic.

Every presidential election asks a fundamental constitutional question: who will exercise the executive power of the United States during the next presidential term? Election Day is central to that decision, but it is not the end of the constitutional process.

From the ballot to the presidency

The American presidential election process moves through institutions established by the Constitution and federal and state law. States administer elections and determine their results. Presidential electors are appointed, the electors vote, electoral certificates are transmitted, Congress counts the electoral votes, and the next presidential term begins.

01 — The People VoteCitizens cast ballots under the election laws administered by their states.
02 — States Determine ResultsVotes are canvassed and state processes resolve certification, recounts, audits, and lawful challenges.
03 — Electors Are AppointedStates appoint presidential electors according to the constitutional and statutory process.
04 — Electors VoteThe appointed electors meet in their states and cast votes for President and Vice President.
05 — Votes Are TransmittedElectoral certificates are prepared and transmitted as required by law.
06 — Congress CountsThe electoral votes are opened and counted in a joint session of Congress.
07 — The Presidential Term BeginsThe Twentieth Amendment establishes the beginning of the presidential term on January 20.
POLITICAL LEADERS MAY CHANGE. THE REPUBLIC CONTINUES.

The transfer of power permits political leadership to change without requiring the constitutional government itself to start over.

The peaceful transfer of power

The Constitution, Congress, the courts, federal agencies, the armed forces under civilian authority, the states, and the legal obligations of government continue beyond any individual presidency. Government power belongs to constitutional offices and institutions; it is not the permanent possession of a president, political party, movement, or faction.

A peaceful transfer of power therefore means more than ceremony. It is evidence that political disagreement can be resolved through constitutional processes while the Republic continues.

Disagreement is part of the Republic

Citizens do not surrender their right to disagree with an election result. Election administration may be questioned. Authorized recounts may be sought. Lawsuits may be filed. Citizens may petition government, criticize public officials, advocate changes in law, and demonstrate peacefully.

The historical question is not whether disagreement is permitted. It is how disagreement is pursued within a constitutional system and what happens when political conflict reaches institutions responsible for completing the transfer of power.

What is January 6?

January 6, 2021 was the date Congress met in joint session to count the electoral votes from the 2020 presidential election under the constitutional and statutory process then in effect. It was not another nationwide presidential election, and Congress was not simply choosing whichever presidential candidate its members preferred.

The Vice President presided over the joint session while electoral certificates were considered and counted. Members of Congress could raise objections through procedures established by the federal law then governing the count.

An important historical distinction

LAW IN EFFECT ON JANUARY 6, 2021 ≠ CURRENT ELECTORAL-COUNT LAW

Congress substantially revised federal electoral-count procedures in 2022. This project will distinguish the rules that governed the 2021 proceeding from reforms enacted afterward and will not retroactively apply today's procedures to the events of January 6.

Then came January 6, 2021

While Congress was conducting the electoral count, the Capitol was breached. The proceeding was interrupted. Members of Congress and the Vice President were moved or protected as law enforcement confronted rioters throughout the Capitol complex.

Later that day, law enforcement regained control of the building. Congress returned, resumed the electoral count, and completed the constitutional proceeding.

The events produced criminal investigations and prosecutions, congressional inquiries, impeachment proceedings, security and intelligence reviews, civil litigation, constitutional disputes, Supreme Court decisions, reforms to electoral-count law, presidential clemency, and continuing public disagreement.

Why create a memory project?

January 6 is frequently presented through competing political narratives. This project begins with a different principle: preserve and organize the record so citizens can examine it themselves.

Allegations will remain identified as allegations unless established through appropriate evidence or adjudication. Court findings will be distinguished from congressional findings. Procedural dismissals will not be described as adjudications of factual questions that a court did not decide. Conflicting evidence will be shown rather than hidden, and later developments will be added when they materially change the record.

The purpose is not to tell citizens what they must believe.

It is to help preserve and organize the record citizens need in order to reach informed conclusions for themselves.

Questions this project will examine

What was supposed to happen? What happened before and during January 6? What evidence establishes those events? What election challenges were raised and how were they resolved? What did government officials know before January 6? Why did security fail? What actions were taken by the President, Vice President, Congress, federal agencies, state officials, law enforcement, political organizations, demonstrators and individuals who entered the Capitol?

What did congressional investigations conclude? What did prosecutors allege? What did courts actually determine? Which claims were never adjudicated? Where does reliable evidence conflict? What remains disputed? What later evidence or legal developments changed the historical record?

What can January 6 teach us about elections, constitutional government, the transfer of power, and the responsibility of citizens to sustain the Republic?
