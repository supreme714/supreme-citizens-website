Founding Documents & Constitutional Resources

Read the texts, study the debates, and distinguish the Constitution itself from historical arguments about its meaning. This page connects visitors to authoritative public sources for constitutional learning.

How these resources fit the Supreme Citizens Charter: The Charter is a civic and educational framework; it is not constitutional law. 1781–1789

Articles of Confederation

The first national governing framework created a confederation in which the states retained substantial authority and the national government had limited powers. Studying its weaknesses helps explain many choices made at the Constitutional Convention.

Authoritative source: National Archives

Read the Articles
1787

U.S. Constitution

The Constitution establishes the federal government's structure, enumerates and distributes powers, creates the Legislative, Executive, and Judicial branches, addresses federal-state relationships, and provides an amendment process.

Official source: National Archives

Read the Constitution
1791

Bill of Rights

The first ten amendments protect fundamental liberties and impose important limits on government, including protections involving expression, religion, due process, criminal procedure, and powers reserved to the states or people.

Official source: National Archives

Read the Bill of Rights

Constitutional development

Amendments 1–27

The Constitution has been amended twenty-seven times. The amendments address individual rights, slavery and citizenship, equal protection, voting, presidential elections and succession, congressional procedures, and other features of constitutional government.

For legal study, Constitution Annotated provides clause-by-clause analysis and discussion of Supreme Court interpretation prepared by the Congressional Research Service.

Official source: Constitution Annotated / Congress.gov

Read Amendments 1–27

Read text and interpretation separately

The constitutional text is the starting point. Historical debates, the Federalist Papers, legislation, executive practice, and judicial decisions can provide context, but they do not all carry the same legal authority. Distinguishing among them is an important part of constitutional literacy.

Ratification-era arguments

The Federalist Papers

The eighty-five essays written under the name “Publius” by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay argued for ratification and explained features of the proposed Constitution. They are influential historical sources for understanding the ratification debate, but they are not themselves constitutional text.

Authoritative historical source: Library of Congress
