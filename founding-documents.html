Study the constitutional tradition

From Confederation to Constitution

The United States developed its constitutional system through a series of foundational documents, debates, compromises, amendments, and interpretations. These resources provide direct access to official and authoritative historical texts.

Readers are encouraged to compare the documents, examine how governmental powers changed over time, and consider how the principles of popular sovereignty, federalism, representation, liberty, and constitutional limits developed.

Recommended study order

1. Articles of Confederation
2. U.S. Constitution
3. Bill of Rights
4. Later Amendments
5. Federalist Papers

1781–1789

Articles of Confederation

The Articles of Confederation served as the first national framework of government for the United States. They created a confederation of sovereign states with a comparatively weak central government.

Read the Articles
1787

U.S. Constitution

The Constitution established the structure of the federal government, divided powers among three branches, defined relationships between the federal government and the states, and created the framework for amendment.

Read the Constitution
1791

Bill of Rights

The first ten amendments protect fundamental liberties and place important limits on government power, including protections involving speech, religion, due process, criminal procedure, and reserved powers.

Read the Bill of Rights

Constitutional development

The Amendments

The Constitution has been amended twenty-seven times. Later amendments abolished slavery, defined citizenship, expanded voting rights, changed presidential and congressional procedures, and addressed other major features of American government.

Studying the amendments together shows how the constitutional system has evolved while retaining its basic framework of representative government and written constitutional law.

Read Amendments 1–27

Major amendment themes

• Individual rights and liberties
• Citizenship and equal protection
• Voting rights
• Presidential elections and succession
• Congressional procedures
• Federal and state powers

The ratification debate

The Federalist Papers

The Federalist Papers are a collection of eighty-five essays written under the name “Publius” by Alexander Hamilton, James Madison, and John Jay. The essays argued for ratification of the Constitution and explained many features of the proposed federal system.

They remain important sources for understanding arguments about federalism, separation of powers, representation, factions, judicial authority, executive power, and republican government.

Read the Federalist Papers
